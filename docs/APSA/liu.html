Devolution’s Dilemma by Yufeng Liu

Devin Judge-Lord

2026-09-02

Why do central governments choose partial devolution?

  • they can’t commit to no bailouts, so they keep control of taxation
  • they don’t trust local governments to spend optimally because they don’t trust accountability to local voters because participation is low (because of no discretion – the circular dilemma)

A fun read

  • the logic seems correct on the margin, though perhaps not the whole story of local autonomy
  • nice integration of prose, case history, formal theory, and empirical tests
  • the small examples early on are helpful; even more would be clarifying
  • clarify how centralized differ from federal regimes in practice (or do parts apply to federal systems too?)
  • long for an article, approaching a short book? Or one formal theory paper and one empirical paper.

Key Assumptions

  1. Local governments spend on clientism (“demographically targeted”), not public goods, when voter participation is low. As participation increases, local spending decisions approach fiscally responsible, (nationally?) socially optimal, and efficient spending, avoiding the need for bailouts.

  2. central governments delegate discretion based on socially optimal and efficient spending, which they infer from voter participation rates (because of #1)

  3. voter participation varies with discretionary spending (stakes)

Result: limited delegation -> limited stakes -> limited participation -> unaccountable spending -> limited delegation

My other comments are from a U.S. perspective

(stronger federalism, but some of the logic should still apply?)

  • no truly discretionary spending different.
  • but lots of “mandated spending” has a lot of discretion (e.g., a mandate to provide transportation for people with disabilities, but how is discretionary.) similar “ad hoc arrangements” (p. 6)
  • local governments can borrow and go bankrupt (different key to this paper)

Causes of local underprovision

  1. caps on own-sourced revenue (centralism’s defining feature #1)
  2. local politics
    • in the U.S., local governments choose not to tax, either because they are poor or rich. Only the central government can redistribute.
    • thus, even under full discretion, we may see similar under-provision
  3. national politics
    • money is power; under fiscal federalism, grants are purpose.
    • are unitary governments’ transfers that different in practice? (defining feature #2)

Politics

“The problem facing today’s states is fundamentally the cost-benefit analysis of maintaining the desirable performance of local state capacity at an acceptable cost…[BUT] institutions are more often the outcomes of political struggles and compromises than the brainchild of maximizing planners” (p. 8)

Central governments are not performance or fiscal responsibility maximizes; they are partisan policy maximizes, increasingly industrial policy coordinators.

Politics - more alternative mechanisms

  • Delivery via local governments is more about discretion via small-government ideology than efficiency - i.e., local governments often make delivery worse

  • Devolution not to partisan allies, but to impose partisan goals, e.g., Ds imposing medicaid expansion on R states

  • Blame shifting (poor delivery, poor enforcement)

Naive empirical questions

How does “tendency to over-spend” AND “under-provide public goods” (p. 8) manifest in practice?

Liu notes the contradiction here. An example would help. Ostrom and others show that theorized tragedies of the commons don’t often occur in practice, and I’m not clear on the theory here. If they can’t borrow, how do they spend more than allocated? If they are under-providing due to clientism, what does this look like?

“how far each is checked depends on to what extent the residents can hold local government accountable.” (p. 8)

Why would residents hold local governments accountable for spending more than nationally socially optimal locally?
